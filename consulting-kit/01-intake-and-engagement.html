01

The idea, in one paragraph

Banks are being told they "need an AI strategy" by every vendor and consultant in the market. Most of that noise skips the first, unglamorous step: honestly assessing what the bank already has, what it's allowed to do with its data, and where the real time is being lost. That's the inspection. You're not selling software — you're telling Ben the truth about the house before anyone spends money on renovations.

Why you're the right inspector

You've sat in the credit shop. You know what spreading a financial statement, writing a credit memo, and prepping for an exam actually take. That means you can spot where AI saves real hours versus where it's a toy — and you can speak to a bank president in his own language. Most "AI consultants" can't tell a call report from a call center.

03

Sorting out "Fiserv or FDIC or OCC"

These are three different things that get jumbled together. Getting them straight is part of what makes you credible in the room — and it changes what the bank is allowed to do.

Who regulates them (not the same as who insures them)

A bank named "Collins State Bank" is almost certainly state-chartered in Wisconsin. That means:

  • Wisconsin DFI (Department of Financial Institutions, Division of Banking) — the state chartering regulator.
  • Plus one federal regulator, depending on Fed membership: FDIC if it's a state non-member bank (the common case for community banks), or the Federal Reserve if it's a state member bank.
  • OCC only regulates national banks — the ones with "National" or "N.A." in the name. A "State Bank" is not OCC-supervised. So it's very likely FDIC + Wisconsin DFI, not OCC.

Separately, FDIC insurance is the deposit insurance nearly every bank carries regardless of who its primary regulator is — that's why "FDIC" gets used as a catch-all. On the intake form you'll simply ask them to confirm charter type and primary federal regulator rather than guess.

Fiserv is the core — a completely different question

Fiserv (you had it as "Fieerv"), Jack Henry, and FIS are the three big core processors — the system of record that runs accounts, loans, and the general ledger. A Wisconsin community bank is most likely on Fiserv (Premier, Precision, or Signature) or Jack Henry (SilverLake, CIF 20/20, Core Director). The single most important thing to learn is whether the core is vendor-hosted / service bureau or in-house — it dictates what data can move and what's even technically possible.

The compliance backdrop you'll reference in the report

You don't need to be a compliance officer, but naming these shows the bank you understand their guardrails: the Interagency Guidance on Third-Party Relationships (FDIC/OCC/Fed, 2023) for any vendor, GLBA / the Safeguards Rule for customer data, the FFIEC IT Handbook, fair-lending rules (ECOA / Reg B) the moment AI touches a credit decision, and BSA/AML if it touches monitoring. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is a good neutral backbone for the whole report.

05

What you actually score

The report rates the bank across six dimensions — this is the backbone that turns a pile of intake answers into a defensible finding. A simple 1–5 per dimension is plenty.

I
Data & infrastructure

Where data lives, hosting model, what's allowed to leave the building.

II
Technology fit

Core and ancillary systems, existing AI features, integration surface.

III
Security & compliance

GLBA program, third-party risk maturity, fair-lending and BSA exposure.

IV
People & change readiness

Sponsor, staff comfort, track record with new tools.

V
Governance

Who decides, board awareness, existing AI / acceptable-use policy.

VI
Opportunity value

Where hours and dollars are genuinely recoverable — the payoff map.

06

What Ben walks away with

  • A plain-English current-state findings report — strengths, gaps, and the shadow-AI reality.
  • A readiness scorecard across the six dimensions above.
  • A prioritized opportunity roadmap — quick wins (low-risk internal productivity) separated from strategic bets (anything touching credit decisions or customer data).
  • A short guardrails recommendation — the AI / acceptable-use policy the bank should have before staff go further.
  • A vendor-landscape snapshot — build vs. buy vs. use-what-you-already-own.
  • An optional next-step proposal for implementation — priced separately, so the inspection stays honest.

Two things to protect yourself

Scope creep & liability. Keep the flat fee tied to the diagnostic only, and put a line in the engagement letter clarifying you're providing advisory recommendations — not legal, compliance, or accounting opinions — and that the bank remains responsible for regulatory decisions. Ask your insurer about professional-liability (E&O) cover before the first paid engagement. Being Ben's former colleague is trust capital; a clean paper trail keeps it that way.
